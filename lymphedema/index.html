Foundations

The Lymphatic System

A one-way drainage network running parallel to the venous system, returning fluid, protein, and immune cells to circulation.

Three jobs

  • Fluid balance — returns 2–4 L/day to circulation
  • Protein transport — the only route back for large proteins
  • Immune surveillance — nodes filter, host lymphocytes

The pathway

  • Initial lymphatics collect interstitial fluid
  • Collectors move lymph via lymphangions
  • Regional nodes filter the load
  • Thoracic duct → subclavian veins
Lymphangion contraction is the primary pump. It is stimulated by stretch, muscle contraction, arterial pulsation, and breathing — which is exactly why exercise and diaphragmatic breathing are treatment, not just adjuncts.
Foundations

Lymph Load vs. Transport Capacity

Every case of lymphedema is a mismatch between how much fluid needs to move and how much the system can move.

The three states

  • Normal — capacity exceeds load, large reserve
  • Dynamic insufficiency — normal vessels, excess load. Ordinary edema.
  • Mechanical insufficiency — damaged vessels. This is lymphedema.

Why the protein matters

  • Retained protein is osmotically active — pulls in water
  • Drives inflammation and fibrosis
  • Fibrosis further cuts capacity — a self-reinforcing loop
Combined insufficiency — high load AND damaged system — is common in the patients you will actually see.
Etiology

Primary vs. Secondary Lymphedema

Primary lymphedema arises from congenital lymphatic dysplasia. Secondary lymphedema follows anatomical disruption of a previously normal system.

Primary — developmental

  • Congenital — birth to age 2, incl. Milroy disease
  • Praecox — puberty to 35. Most common primary form.
  • Tarda — after 35

Secondary — acquired

  • Cancer treatment — node dissection and/or radiation. Leading cause here.
  • Filariasis — leading cause worldwide
  • Trauma, surgery, burns
  • Chronic venous insufficiency → phlebolymphedema
  • Recurrent infection and obesity
Risk multipliers after cancer treatment: extent of node dissection, regional radiation, elevated BMI, post-op infection, wound complications, and taxane-based chemotherapy.
Presentation

Symptomatology

Subjective symptoms frequently precede visible swelling by months. Do not wait for a measurable difference to take a patient seriously.

Early — often reported before anything is visible

  • Heaviness, fullness, tightness
  • Clothing, rings or shoes fitting differently
  • Aching without a mechanical cause
  • Swelling that resolves overnight

Established

  • Persistent swelling, no longer resolving with elevation
  • Pitting → non-pitting as fibrosis develops
  • Skin thickened and firm
  • Positive Stemmer's sign
  • Recurrent cellulitis

Do not overlook

  • Psychosocial burden — body image, social withdrawal
  • Functional loss — grip, reach, gait, footwear
  • Occupational impact and garment cost
Lymphedema is typically painless early. New or significant pain suggests infection, DVT, malignancy — or lipedema, which is painful by definition.
Assessment

Diagnosis and Measurement

Diagnosis is primarily clinical. History and physical examination identify the great majority of cases.

History

  • Onset, duration, whether it resolves overnight
  • Surgical and oncologic history — nodes, radiation fields
  • Every episode of cellulitis
  • Family history; travel history

Physical examination

  • Stemmer's sign — cannot lift the skin fold = positive = lymphedema
  • Pitting — depth and rebound, graded 1+ to 4+
  • Skin — texture, papillomas, fissures, fungal infection
  • Compare sides — symmetry, foot involvement

Objective measurement

  • Circumferential tape at 4 cm intervals — cheap, reliable
  • Volumetry — water displacement
  • Perometry — infrared, fast and reproducible
  • Bioimpedance (L-Dex) — can detect Stage 0

Imaging — when the picture is unclear

  • Lymphoscintigraphy — the imaging standard
  • ICG lymphography — maps superficial vessels
  • Duplex US — rule out DVT
  • MRI / CT — rule out malignancy
Severity by volume difference vs. the contralateral limb: minimal <20%, moderate 20–40%, severe >40%.

Staging

ISL Staging — Overview

The International Society of Lymphology defines four stages. Stage 0 is latent — the lymphatics are already impaired before anything is visible.

Staging describes tissue condition, not size. A small Stage III limb is still Stage III. Severity is graded separately by volume difference.
Staging
Stage 0

Latent / Subclinical

Lymph transport is already impaired. No visible swelling. May persist for months or years before progressing.

Findings

  • No visible or palpable edema
  • Subjective heaviness, tightness, or fullness may be reported
  • Reduced transport capacity detectable by bioimpedance or lymphoscintigraphy
  • Normal tissue consistency
  • Negative Stemmer's sign

PT implications

  • This is the highest-value window for intervention
  • Baseline measurement pre-operatively makes this stage detectable at all
  • Surveillance program, risk-reduction education, early compression if indicated
  • Prophylactic PT after node dissection has been shown to reduce progression

Staging
Stage I

Spontaneously Reversible

Early accumulation of protein-rich fluid. Swelling reduces with elevation — often resolving completely overnight.

Findings

  • Soft, pitting edema
  • Subsides with elevation; frequently gone by morning
  • No secondary tissue changes — no fibrosis yet
  • Stemmer's sign usually negative
  • Limb feels heavy; may be visibly larger by end of day

PT implications

  • Highly responsive to CDT
  • Compression garments often sufficient for maintenance
  • Patient education on self-management is decisive here
  • Goal: prevent the transition to fibrosis

Staging
Stage II

Spontaneously Irreversible

Elevation alone no longer reduces the swelling. Lymphostatic fibrosis has begun and the tissue is changing character.

Findings

  • Elevation rarely reduces the limb
  • Early Stage II — pitting is manifest and pronounced
  • Late Stage II — pitting may be absent as fibrosis replaces fluid
  • Tissue feels firm; Stemmer's sign becomes positive
  • Increasing frequency of cellulitis
  • Skin thickening becomes apparent

PT implications

  • Full intensive-phase CDT is indicated
  • Fibrosis requires manual work plus foam/chip padding under bandaging
  • Realistic goal is volume reduction and stabilization, not cure
  • Aggressive infection-prevention education
The presence or absence of pitting is what separates early from late Stage II — not the size of the limb.
Staging
Stage III

Lymphostatic Elephantiasis

Extreme volume increase with characteristic trophic skin changes. Pitting is typically absent.

Findings

  • Marked volume increase and limb distortion
  • Pitting absent
  • Trophic skin changes — acanthosis, hyperkeratosis
  • Papillomas and warty overgrowth
  • Deep skin folds, fat deposition
  • Lymphorrhea — weeping of lymph fluid through the skin
  • Strongly positive Stemmer's sign
  • Frequent, sometimes life-threatening infection

PT implications

  • Extended intensive phase; often requires wound care alongside CDT
  • Meaningful volume reduction is still achievable — do not write these patients off
  • Multilayer bandaging with substantial padding
  • Coordinate with physician; surgical options may be considered after conservative management
Stage III is not a treatment failure to be managed passively. Significant reduction is well documented with sustained CDT.
Treatment

Complete Decongestive Therapy

CDT is the internationally accepted standard of care. It has four components, delivered in two phases.

Phase I — Intensive / Reductive

  • Typically 3–6 weeks, treatment 4–5x/week, sessions 1–2 hours
  • MLD, multilayer short-stretch bandaging worn 23 hours/day, exercise, skin care
  • Re-measure at every visit
  • Ends when volume plateaus across consecutive sessions

Phase II — Maintenance / Self-Management

  • Fitted compression garment during the day; night garment or bandaging as needed
  • Self-MLD and home exercise program
  • Daily skin care and self-inspection
  • Ongoing surveillance; garments replaced every 4–6 months
  • Return for a short intensive phase if the limb regresses

Treatment · CDT 1 of 4

Skin & Nail Care

A lymphedematous limb is immunocompromised tissue. The skin is the last barrier, and every infection causes further lymphatic damage.

Daily routine

  • Wash with low-pH, fragrance-free cleanser — lymphedematous skin is alkaline-shifted
  • Dry thoroughly, especially between the toes and in skin folds
  • Apply low-pH emollient to keep the barrier intact
  • Inspect the entire limb daily, including between digits and the posterior surface

Prevention

  • Treat tinea pedis and onychomycosis aggressively — a common cellulitis entry point
  • Nails cut straight across; avoid cutting cuticles
  • Avoid blood draws, IVs, and BP cuffs on the at-risk limb where an alternative exists
  • Protect during gardening, cooking, pet handling; gloves and insect repellent
  • Prompt antiseptic care for any break in the skin

Teach the patient to recognize cellulitis

  • Spreading redness, heat, tenderness, sudden limb enlargement
  • Fever, chills, malaise, flu-like onset
  • Seek care same-day. Stop MLD and compression until treated.
  • Recurrent cellulitis may warrant prophylactic antibiotics — physician decision
Treatment · CDT 2 of 4

Manual Lymphatic Drainage

A specific, very light manual technique that stimulates lymphangion contraction and redirects lymph across watersheds into functioning territories.

Technique principles

  • Very light pressure — skin stretch only
  • ~1 stroke/second — matches lymphangion rate
  • Clear proximal before distal
  • Skin moves with the hand — no lubricant, no gliding

Sequence for an affected limb

  • Diaphragmatic breathing
  • Terminus, then trunk quadrants
  • Anastomoses
  • Proximal limb, then distal

Contraindications

  • Acute cellulitis or infection
  • Acute DVT
  • Decompensated heart failure
  • Caution: active malignancy — coordinate with oncology

Treatment · CDT 3 of 4

Compression Therapy

The single most important component for maintaining reduction. Without compression, gains from MLD reverse within hours.

Phase I — short-stretch bandaging

  • Short-stretch — low resting, high working pressure
  • Never long-stretch/ACE — tourniquet risk
  • Layered: stockinette, digits, padding, bandage
  • Foam over fibrosis; gradient higher distally
  • Worn 23 hrs/day

Phase II — garments

  • Class 1 (20–30) — upper limb, mild, prophylaxis
  • Class 2 (30–40) — most lower limb
  • Class 3 (40–50) — severe lower limb
  • Flat-knit preferred; custom for irregular shape
  • Replace every 4–6 months

Precautions

  • Check ABI. < 0.8 caution, < 0.5 contraindicated
  • Decompensated heart failure
  • Acute DVT, acute infection, severe neuropathy
Intermittent pneumatic compression may be a useful adjunct but does not replace MLD or bandaging, and must not be used over an untreated proximal obstruction.
Treatment · CDT 4 of 4

Remedial Exercise

The skeletal muscle pump is a primary driver of lymph transport. Exercise performed under compression is decongestive therapy.

Principles

  • Always with compression in place
  • Start with diaphragmatic breathing
  • Proximal to distal
  • Slow and rhythmic; no Valsalva
  • Low resistance, higher repetition

Programme components

  • Diaphragmatic breathing to start and finish
  • Cervical and shoulder girdle mobility
  • Trunk and core activation
  • Active ROM, proximal → distal
  • Aerobic — walking, cycling, aquatic

The resistance training question — settled

  • Progressive resistance training does not cause or worsen lymphedema
  • The PAL trial demonstrated safety
  • May reduce flare incidence
  • Do not tell patients to avoid using the limb

Treatment · Long-term

Long-Term Compression Garments

Phase II is where the result is held. The garment has to fit the limb, the diagnosis, and the patient's hands — a garment that cannot be donned will not be worn.

Lower limb options

  • Knee-high — most common; easiest to don, best adherence
  • Thigh-high — swelling above the knee; needs a silicone band
  • Pantyhose / waist-high — bilateral or genital involvement
  • Capri — thigh and knee, spared calf. Common in lipedema.

Velcro adjustable wraps

  • Inelastic, overlapping Velcro straps
  • Patient-adjustable through the day
  • Far easier with arthritis, weak grip, or limited reach
  • Bridges Phase I bandaging to a fitted garment
  • Juzo Wrap, Jobst FarrowWrap, Circaid

Flat-knit vs. circular-knit

  • Flat-knit — seamed, made to measure, stiffer. Preferred for lymphedema.
  • Circular-knit — thinner, more cosmetic, tourniquets at folds
  • Any distortion, fibrosis, or deep folds → flat-knit

Custom garments — when off-the-shelf will not do

  • Measurements outside standard sizing
  • Marked distortion, deep folds, lobules
  • Toe caps, gauntlets, gloves, sleeves
  • Genital, breast, head and neck, trunk
  • 2–3 weeks to fabricate

Practical prescribing

  • Two garments so one is always clean
  • Replace every 4–6 months — elastic fatigues before it looks worn
  • Check arterial status first; ABI < 0.8 caution
  • Donning aids: rubber gloves, donner, slip-sock

Lipedema

Lipedema — A Different Disease

A chronic disorder of adipose tissue, almost exclusively affecting women, routinely misdiagnosed as obesity or lymphedema for years before recognition.

Defining features

  • Bilateral and symmetrical
  • Painful — tender to pressure. Diagnostic, not incidental.
  • Bruises easily
  • Spares the feet — sharp ankle cuff
  • Diet-resistant; negative Stemmer's

Etiology

  • Cause unknown; strong familial pattern
  • Hormonally triggered — puberty, pregnancy, menopause
  • Evidence points to a connective tissue component
  • Reported in up to ~11% of women

Why the misdiagnosis matters

  • Patients are told to lose weight — it does not work
  • Years of shame and ineffective treatment
  • Delay allows progression to lymphatic involvement
  • A correct diagnosis is itself therapeutic
Sarah Bramblette and patient advocacy groups have driven recent recognition, but average time to diagnosis remains measured in years.
Lipedema

Lipedema Staging

Classically staged 1 through 3 by skin and tissue architecture. A fourth stage — lipo-lymphedema — is widely used clinically but is contested.

Stage 1

Smooth skin surface

  • Skin surface normal and smooth
  • Enlarged hypodermis; homogeneous thickening of subcutaneous tissue
  • Fat is often soft; small nodules may be palpable — described as rice-like or pearl-sized
  • Fat accumulation around pelvis, buttocks, and hips
  • Limbs tender and prone to bruising
  • Swelling may improve with rest and elevation
Stage 2

Uneven, dimpled skin

  • Skin surface becomes bumpy and wave-like with indentations
  • Nodular structures in the thickened subcutis — lipomas, angiolipomas
  • Subcutaneous tissue feels tougher and more nodular
  • Fat build-up extends down the legs toward the ankles
  • Swelling less responsive to rest and elevation than Stage 1
  • Symptoms range from mild to severe
Stage 3

Large deformations

  • Further hardening of connective tissue
  • Bulky, overhanging extrusions of skin and fat
  • Marked deformation of thighs and around the knees
  • Persistent swelling not resolving with rest or elevation
  • Mobility significantly impaired; highest rate of orthopedic complication
  • May be associated with secondary lymphedema
Stage 4

Lipo-lymphedema

  • Lipedema plus secondary lymphedema in the same limbs
  • The feet begin to swell — the defining change
  • Stemmer's sign becomes positive
  • Skin over the toes thickens; pitting appears
  • Diagnosis moves from lipedema to lipo-lymphedema
  • Lymphoscintigraphy can document the degree of drainage impairment
IMPORTANT CAVEAT: The Lipedema Foundation no longer uses a 'Stage 4' designation, on the grounds that the lymphatic system can be compromised at any stage — lymphatic impairment is documented in Stages 2 and 3. Present Stage 4 as lipo-lymphedema, and note the debate. This is the intellectually honest framing and it will be the question a sharp student asks.

Lipedema

PT Management of Lipedema

Conservative management does not remove lipedema fat. It manages pain, controls fluid, preserves mobility, and slows progression.

What PT can achieve

  • Pain reduction — often the patient's priority
  • Control of the fluid component
  • Preserve joint mechanics and gait
  • Prevent progression to lipo-lymphedema

Interventions

  • Flat-knit compression
  • MLD for fluid and for pain
  • Aquatic exercise — buoyancy plus hydrostatic compression
  • Low-impact aerobic and resistance work
  • Nutritional referral

Set expectations honestly

  • Conservative care does not remove the fat
  • Liposuction is the only intervention that does
  • PT before and after surgery improves outcomes
  • Insurance coverage remains inconsistent
The single most valuable thing you may do is tell the patient this is a recognized disease and not a personal failure of willpower.
Beyond CDT

Other Considerations After Lymphatic PT

Decongestion is not the end of the episode of care. Once volume is controlled, what remains is often what limits the patient most.

Gait training

  • Gait adapted around a heavy limb persists after decongestion
  • Reassess step length, cadence, base of support
  • Shoe size often changes after Phase I
  • Calf pump is both a gait and a lymphatic goal
  • Review the assistive device

Balance therapy

  • Altered limb mass and distal sensation degrade postural control
  • Screen falls risk — rarely done in this population
  • Static → dynamic, then dual-task
  • Proprioceptive retraining at ankle and hip
  • A fall breaking skin is a cellulitis risk

Orthopedic pain not relieved by CDT

  • If pain persists once volume is controlled, treat it as a separate problem
  • Knee and hip OA; low back pain from gait asymmetry
  • Post-axillary-dissection shoulder — capsule, scar, cording
  • Plantar fasciitis from changed weight-bearing
  • Disproportionate bilateral tenderness → consider lipedema

Ongoing surveillance

  • Re-measure at regular intervals; catch regression early
  • Garment replacement every 4–6 months
  • Short repeat intensive phase if the limb regresses — this is expected, not failure
  • Annual skin and nail review
  • Re-screen falls risk and function, not just limb volume

Safety

Red Flags & Referral

Stop treatment and refer when you see these.

Urgent — same day

  • Cellulitis — spreading erythema, heat, fever
  • Suspected DVT — acute unilateral swelling, calf pain
  • Decompensated heart failure — dyspnea, orthopnea
  • Systemic illness — fever, rigors, hypotension

Refer for workup

  • Rapid-onset or proximal-first swelling — possible malignancy
  • New neurological deficit
  • Non-healing wound or ulceration
  • Failure to respond to well-delivered CDT

Screen before starting

  • ABI before lower limb compression
  • Cardiac and renal status
  • Active malignancy — coordinate with oncology
  • Peripheral neuropathy and sensory testing
MLD is contraindicated in acute infection, acute DVT, and decompensated heart failure. When in doubt, do not treat — refer.

Key Takeaways

  1. Lymphedema is mechanical insufficiency — high-protein, progressive, and self-reinforcing if untreated.
  2. Symptoms precede visible swelling. Stage 0 is real and is the best time to intervene.
  3. Stemmer's sign is fast, free, and diagnostic when positive.
  4. Four ISL stages: 0 latent, I reversible, II irreversible/fibrotic, III elephantiasis.
  5. CDT has four components. Compression is the one that holds the result.
  6. Exercise under compression is treatment. Resistance training is safe.
  7. Garment choice is decided as much by whether the patient can don it as by pressure class.
  8. Lipedema is painful, symmetrical, spares the feet, and has a negative Stemmer.
  9. Lipedema is staged 1–3, with lipo-lymphedema as a contested Stage 4.
  10. Every episode of cellulitis makes the underlying disease worse.
  11. Patient education and Phase II adherence determine long-term outcome.
  12. Decongestion is not discharge — reassess gait, balance, and orthopedic pain afterwards.
