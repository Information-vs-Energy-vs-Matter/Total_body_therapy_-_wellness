Self-check

12 questions. Think it through, then tap to reveal.

1A patient reports her arm feels heavy and her rings are tight, but you can see no swelling and measurements match the other side. What ISL stage is this, and does it need treatment?
Stage 0 — latent/subclinical. Lymph transport is already impaired even though nothing is visible. Yes, it needs attention: this is the highest-value window for intervention. Start surveillance, risk-reduction education, and consider early compression.
2You attempt to pinch the skin at the base of the second toe and cannot lift a fold. What is this sign, and what does it tell you?
A positive Stemmer's sign. It is diagnostic for lymphedema. Note the asymmetry of the test: a positive result confirms lymphedema, but a negative result does NOT rule it out.
3What single finding most reliably separates early Stage II from late Stage II?
Whether the tissue pits. Early Stage II shows manifest pitting; in late Stage II pitting may be absent as lymphostatic fibrosis replaces fluid. Limb size does not determine the stage.
4Why is heavy pressure wrong during manual lymphatic drainage?
Initial lymphatics are blind-ended, thin-walled vessels anchored by filaments. Heavy pressure collapses them, blocking uptake. MLD uses pressure light enough only to stretch the skin, at roughly one stroke per second to match lymphangion contraction rate.
5Why must you clear proximal territories before working distally?
Fluid has to have somewhere to go. If the trunk quadrant and terminus are still congested, mobilizing distal fluid simply pushes it into a full receiving area. Always empty the receiving territory first.
6Why short-stretch and never long-stretch (ACE) bandages?
Short-stretch gives low resting pressure and high working pressure — it resists the muscle during contraction but does not constrict at rest. Long-stretch has high resting pressure and can act as a tourniquet over the 23 hours a day the bandage is worn.
7A patient with lower-limb lymphedema has an ABI of 0.6. What do you do?
Do not apply standard compression. ABI below 0.8 requires caution and below 0.5 is a contraindication. Arterial insufficiency means compression can compromise perfusion. Refer for vascular assessment and coordinate on modified or reduced compression.
8A breast cancer survivor with Stage I arm lymphedema asks whether she should stop lifting weights. What is the evidence-based answer?
No. Slowly progressive resistance training does not cause or worsen lymphedema, and the PAL trial demonstrated safety in this exact population. It may reduce flares. Progress gradually, wear compression during exercise, and monitor for symptom change. Advising limb disuse is outdated and causes deconditioning.
9Bilateral symmetrical leg swelling, painful and tender, bruises easily, stops abruptly at the ankles with normal-looking feet, negative Stemmer. Diagnosis?
Lipedema. The combination of pain, symmetry, easy bruising, foot-sparing with a sharp ankle cuff, and a negative Stemmer's sign distinguishes it from lymphedema.
10That same patient returns two years later. Her feet are now swelling, Stemmer is positive, and the skin over her toes has thickened. What changed?
She has developed lipo-lymphedema — secondary lymphedema on top of her lipedema, commonly called Stage 4. Foot involvement and a positive Stemmer are the defining transition. Note that the Lipedema Foundation no longer uses the Stage 4 label, since lymphatic compromise can occur at any stage.
11Name the three absolute contraindications to MLD.
Acute cellulitis or active infection, acute DVT, and decompensated congestive heart failure. Acute renal failure is also a contraindication, and active untreated malignancy requires coordination with oncology.
12A patient in Phase II calls: her leg is suddenly larger, hot, red, and she feels feverish. What do you tell her?
Seek medical care the same day — this is likely cellulitis. Stop MLD and compression until it is treated. Every episode causes further lymphatic damage, so recurrent cellulitis may warrant prophylactic antibiotics, which is a physician decision.
Answers reflect the ISL 2023 consensus and current lipedema staging. Where the literature is genuinely divided — notably lipedema Stage 4 — the answer says so.
